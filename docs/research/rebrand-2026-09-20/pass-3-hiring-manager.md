# Pass 3 — Hiring manager read on Sumaiya

I run a mid-size company in Dar. I've read thousands of these things. I'm not
writing as a UX person or a growth person — I'm writing as the person on the
other end of the CV, deciding in about ninety seconds whether to move someone
forward.

## 1. A candidate tells me they used this app — what do I now believe?

Honestly, my first reaction is mildly negative, and I want to explain exactly
why so it's actionable rather than just a vibe.

Format tools are not new to me. Every year I get more CVs that look
professionally templated and say less. A clean template with an AI assistant
behind it raises my prior that the wording has been smoothed by something
other than the candidate — generic verbs, generic "results-driven" framing,
achievements that read like they were reverse-engineered from the job posting
rather than lived. That's not a guess about this product specifically, it's
what template-plus-assistant tools do to CVs in general in my experience.
JUDGEMENT, but an informed one — I see the pattern weekly.

That's the hurt side. Here's the help side, and it's real: a candidate who
used a *Tanzanian jobs feed*, ran a *career interview* to build their profile,
and used an *on-device readability check* before sending it to me has done
more preparation than the median entry-level applicant I see, who sends me a
CV that was clearly typed once in Word in 2019 and never touched again. If I
can tell the CV was tailored to *my* posting — not just formatted nicely, but
actually responsive to what I asked for — that's a positive signal regardless
of what tool produced it. The tool is invisible to me at that point; the fit
is what I'm reading.

So: neutral-to-slightly-negative on the mention alone, because "I used an app"
tells me about the process, not the person. What actually moves my opinion is
whether the CV in front of me reads as specific to my job or as generically
polished. The app is only as good, to me, as the specificity of what a
candidate did with it — and that's mostly not under the app's control.

## 2. Is "CV inayokufikisha kwa mwajiri" — "the CV that reaches the employer" — honest?

As the employer, no, and it's not a small quibble. "Reaches the employer" is a
promise about *me* — that my inbox, my ATS, my hiring process is the thing
being solved for. But nothing in this product's real feature list (CV/export,
jobs feed, readability check, application tracking, career interview,
learning hub) touches my side of the pipe. There's no evidence here of
integration with employer systems, no distribution channel into companies
like mine, nothing that reaches me differently than a PDF emailed the old
way. What actually "reaches" is still the candidate hitting send.

What the claim is really describing, generously, is "a CV that clears
automated screening and reads well to a human" — i.e., a *readiness* claim,
not a *delivery* claim. Those are different promises. Readiness I can believe:
the on-device CV check and templates plausibly make a CV less likely to get
auto-rejected for formatting reasons, or less likely to bore a tired reviewer
on CV #140 of the day. Delivery — the CV actually reaching me, as opposed to
being screened before I ever see it — is not something a CV-builder controls.

If I were advising the product, I'd want something closer to "the CV that
doesn't get thrown out before anyone reads it" — a claim about surviving the
first cut, not reaching a destination it doesn't control. Something like
"CV inayosomeka, si kutupwa" (a CV that gets read, not discarded) is honest in
a way "reaches the employer" isn't. I'd rather a tool undersell and overdeliver
with me than the reverse — the first time I catch a product overclaiming, I
discount everything else it says too.

## 3. Application tracking and follow-up nudges — good for candidates, good for me, or both?

Genuinely good for candidates, mixed for me, and I want to be specific about
the mixed part rather than just saying "it depends."

Good for candidates: most first-time jobseekers I meet have no system. They
apply, they forget where, they don't follow up because they don't know
whether it's appropriate or how. A tool that keeps their own list straight is
pure upside for them and costs me nothing.

Where it gets uncomfortable is the *nudge* half. A nudge that reminds a
candidate "it's been two weeks, maybe check in" is fine — that's normal,
expected professional behavior and I don't mind a polite follow-up email.
What I would dislike, and what I've already seen from other markets where
these tools got popular, is templated, timer-driven follow-ups that arrive on
a schedule rather than out of genuine interest — five people all emailing me
on day 14 because an app told them to, using near-identical phrasing. That
reads as automation, not persistence, and it actually *lowers* my opinion of
the candidate because it signals the outreach isn't really theirs. JUDGEMENT:
I haven't seen this specific app's nudge copy, so I can't say whether it
triggers this — but the mechanism (scheduled nudges at scale) is exactly the
kind of thing that produces synchronized, generic follow-up if the nudge
copy isn't varied and the timing isn't left to candidate judgment.

Net: good for candidates individually, mildly bad for me if it's uniform
across many candidates from the same app. The fix, if the product cares, is
to nudge candidates to write their *own* follow-up rather than templating
one, and to vary timing rather than firing on a fixed clock.

## 4. If this became common among entry-level candidates in Tanzania

What I'd start to see: a compression of the *visible* quality band. Right
now, formatting quality tells me something — someone who successfully
structured a clean CV usually also successfully structured their thoughts on
the job posting. If everyone's baseline formatting converges because everyone
used the same 47 templates, that signal disappears. I stop being able to use
"this CV looks professional" as any kind of filter, because it stops
correlating with effort or care — it just correlates with owning a phone with
this app on it.

What I'd start to discount: template polish, generically strong summary
paragraphs, keyword-matched skills sections. Those become table stakes, worth
nothing, the way a spellchecked CV is worth nothing today — it's not a
positive signal, its *absence* is just a negative one.

What I'd start to weight more, correspondingly: anything that's hard to
templatize. Specific project details, numbers a bot wouldn't invent, evidence
of actually reading and responding to *my* posting rather than a generic
version of the role, and — increasingly — the interview itself, because
that's the one part of the pipeline these tools can't smooth over for
someone.

The failure mode, named plainly: a CV tool that makes everyone legible makes
everyone interchangeable. The tool's value to the *individual* candidate
(look competent) inversely erodes its value to the *cohort* (stand out) as
adoption grows — that's not a flaw specific to this app, it's what happens to
any signal that gets easy to fake at scale. The candidates worst hurt by this
are the genuinely good ones who now look identical to everyone else in the
pile, and the candidates who most benefit are the weak ones who get a free
pass through the first filter they'd otherwise have failed on formatting
alone. That's a real cost, and it's worth the product being honest about
rather than pretending "professional-looking CV" is the whole game.

## 5. What should the product lead with?

I don't think it can please employers and candidates with the *same* headline,
and I don't think it should try — trying usually produces a claim, like the
current one, that's vague enough to not quite be true for either audience.

For candidates, the honest and still compelling lead is readiness and
completeness of the *process*, not the document: "prepare properly for the
job you want" — the jobs feed, the interview practice, the readability check,
the tracking, all as one coherent thing, not "AI CV maker" first with
everything else buried. That's actually the more interesting story and the
one that's true today, per the brief: it's a career-prep workspace, not a
document factory.

For employers, if the product ever wants my trust specifically — and I don't
think it needs to, most CV tools never talk to me — it would have to lead
with something about the *floor* it raises, not the ceiling it fakes: fewer
CVs that are unreadable garbage, more that are structured enough to evaluate
fast. Not "this CV reaches you," but something closer to "helps candidates
show up prepared" — a claim about behavior, not outcome, which is the only
kind of claim a prep tool can actually back.

If forced to pick one audience to lead with: candidates. They're who opens
the app. I'd rather the headline earn their trust honestly (readiness, not
delivery) than chase a mixed claim that's slightly false to both of us.
