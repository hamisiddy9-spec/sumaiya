## Part 1 — How do African-market products talk to their users?

Find and read the homepages, app-store listings and campaign copy of products built FOR African consumers
(not for African enterprises, and not global products with an African office). Try: M-Pesa (Vodacom Tanzania /
Safaricom), Tigo Pesa / Mixx by Yas, Airtel Money, Tala, Branch, Moniepoint, OPay, Kuda, PiggyVest,
Wave (Senegal), Flutterwave, mDoc, Kasha, Copia, Jumia, Twiga Foods, Sun King, MKOPA, Zola Electric, and any
2025–26 African consumer launch you find. Include at least two products whose copy is in Swahili.

Answer:
1. **Register.** Formal or spoken? Do they use second person singular, imperatives, or communal "we"? Do they
   use proverbs, or is that a cliché nobody actually uses? Give exact quoted examples with URLs.
2. **What they claim.** Outcome, access, dignity, price, speed? Which of those appears most and which least?
3. **Swahili copy specifically.** When a product ships real Swahili (not machine translation), what does it
   look like? Quote it. Is it short sentences, officialese, or everyday speech?
4. **Low-bandwidth literacy conventions.** Do African consumer products write for a reader who is
   half-reading, on a small screen, possibly with a second language? What do they do about it — sentence
   length, numbers, avoiding jargon, naming things in full? Give examples.
5. **What is embarrassed/avoided.** Anything a well-run African consumer brand conspicuously does not do.
6. **The anti-patterns.** Find examples of African-market copy that is obviously written for a foreign
   audience or a donor/grant rather than the user — "empowering", "leveraging technology", "bridging the
   digital divide", "unlocking potential". Quote real examples with URLs and say what makes them fail.

## Part 2 — Tone when a product is a memorial

This part is delicate and must be handled with restraint. Context: a founder named a career product after his
late sister. The product is, among other things, a memorial to her. It is a working product for jobseekers in
Tanzania — not a foundation, not a charity, not a scholarship.

Research, with sources, and answer:
1. **How do companies that carry a deceased person's name handle it?** Find real, documented cases — a company
   named for a late family member, a brand honouring a deceased co-founder, a memorial product. How much of it
   do they say publicly, and where (homepage, About page, footer, nothing at all)? Cite what you find.
2. **Is there a documented rule or professional consensus** about disclosing a personal tragedy in consumer
   brand copy — from brand strategists, grief counsellors writing for business, or founders who have written
   about it? If no consensus exists, say so rather than assembling one from blog posts.
3. **What are the failure modes?** Where does saying it publicly go wrong — turning grief into marketing,
   making the user feel obliged, inviting sympathy instead of trust, or making a benefit-driven product look
   like a charity case? Find real cases of the failure mode if they exist.
4. **What does it mean for VOICE rather than copy?** If a memorial shapes how the product speaks — dignity,
   warmth, no hype, no gimmicks, nothing that would embarrass her name — what concrete, checkable rules follow?
   Translate the sentiment into rules someone could enforce: words to avoid, sentence shapes to use, claims to
   refuse, tone under failure/error states, tone in celebration states.
5. **Your recommendation.** For a public landing page read by a stranger in Dar es Salaam who knows nothing
   about the founder: should the memorial be stated, hinted, or unstated? If stated, draft the exact wording
   (English and Swahili) and say where on the page it belongs. If unstated, say why, and say where it *does*
   belong instead. Be explicit that this is a recommendation for the owner to accept or reject.
