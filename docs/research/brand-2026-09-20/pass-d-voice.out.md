# African-Market Brand Voice — Research Pass D

## Part 1 — How African-market products talk to their users

### 1. Register: formal or spoken? Second person? Proverbs?

The dominant register across surveyed products is **direct, spoken, second-person-singular, imperative** — closer to a shopkeeper talking to one customer than an institution addressing a public. Examples:

- Wave: "**Deposit, withdraw, pay bills for free. Send for only 1%.**" — imperative verbs stacked with no subject, reading like spoken instruction. [wave.com](https://www.wave.com/en/)
- Kuda: "**Send money fast, spend with confidence, and save automatically with Kuda.**" Same imperative-stack pattern. [kuda.com](https://kuda.com/)
- Moniepoint's Nigerian-Pidgin product line: "**POS machine wey no get wahala**" ("a POS machine that gives no trouble") — a full switch into Pidgin register for a specific product tile, not a gloss of English copy. This is the clearest evidence in the sample of a brand using the customer's actual spoken register rather than "African-inflected English." [moniepoint.com/ng](https://moniepoint.com/ng)
- Sun King (Tanzania): "**Hello, Tanzania!**" as a homepage headline — direct address, informal, singular implied reader. [tz.sunking.com](https://tz.sunking.com/)

**Communal "we"** appears mainly in institutional self-description, not in the offer copy: M-KOPA's "**Trusted by over 10 million customers across five markets**" and "**Our impact begins with unlocking financial inclusion**" both use "we/our" only when talking about the company's scale, never when telling the user what to do. [m-kopa.com](https://m-kopa.com/)

**Proverbs**: none found in any live product copy in this sample — not on M-Pesa, Wave, Kuda, Moniepoint, PiggyVest, Sun King, Flutterwave, or OPay homepages or app-store listings. The one piece of proverb-adjacent language found was a single Swahili phrase used as a named repayment plan — "**Lipa Pole Pole**" (pay little by little) on Sun King Tanzania — which functions as a product name, not a rhetorical flourish. [tz.sunking.com](https://tz.sunking.com/) Given the brief's suspicion that proverb-use is a cliché nobody actually uses: **confirmed for this sample** — no working consumer product reaches for a methali on its homepage or app description. Where local-language color appears, it's a short functional phrase (a plan name, a Pidgin product descriptor), not folk wisdom.

### 2. What they claim: outcome, access, dignity, price, speed?

Ranking by frequency across the sample:

1. **Speed / ease** — most common. "Apply in 5 min" (Tala), "instant" appears in OPay's core claim "Send and receive money instantly... 100% success rate," Wave's "Buy airtime instantly." [tala.co.ke](https://tala.co.ke/) [OPay App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/ng/app/opay-beyond-banking/id1463776084)
2. **Price** — second most common, and usually stated as a specific number, not a vague "affordable": Wave's "**for only 1%**" and "**for FREE**" repeated three times on one homepage; OPay's cashback percentages; Kuda's "**20% per year**" and "**25 free transfers**." [wave.com](https://www.wave.com/en/) [kuda.com](https://kuda.com/)
3. **Access / scale as trust signal** — "8 million customers," "10 million customers," "5 million business owners" — access is claimed retrospectively (this many people already trust it) rather than aspirationally (this will give you access). [kuda.com](https://kuda.com/) [m-kopa.com](https://m-kopa.com/) [Moniepoint homepage](https://moniepoint.com/ng)
4. **Outcome** (savings goals achieved, business grown) — present but usually the second line under a speed/price headline, e.g., PiggyVest's "**helps over 6 million customers achieve their financial goals**" follows the primary headline "The better way to save & invest online." [piggyvest.com](https://www.piggyvest.com/)
5. **Dignity** — least common as an explicit claim. It shows up structurally rather than as a word: Kasha's "**delivered in plain, anonymous packages**" for reproductive-health products is dignity expressed as a product design detail (privacy), not a stated value. [Fast Company via Kasha coverage](https://www.fastcompany.com/40508215/this-company-delivers-reproductive-health-products-directly-to-women-in-the-developing-world) M-KOPA is the one outlier that states dignity-adjacent language directly ("**Put the Power in Your Hands**"), but it sits next to more donor-toned lines like "unlocking financial inclusion" — see §6.

### 3. Swahili copy specifically

Real (non-machine-translated) Swahili copy found in this sample skews toward **short functional phrases**, not full sentences of flowing prose:

- Sun King Tanzania: "**Lipa Pole Pole**" — colloquial, everyday speech (pole pole = "slowly/little by little," a phrase used in ordinary conversation, not officialese). [tz.sunking.com](https://tz.sunking.com/)
- Airtel Money Tanzania (spokesperson quote used in market press coverage of the "Africa Bila Mipaka" launch): "**Airtel siku zote tumekuwa tukija na huduma bunifu na ambazo zinagusa maisha ya kila siku ya wateja wetu**" ("Airtel has always come up with innovative services that touch our customers' everyday lives") — this is closer to formal corporate Swahili (siku zote, huduma bunifu) than street speech, and it comes from a press-quote context rather than app UI copy. [michuzi.co.tz](https://www.michuzi.co.tz/2022/11/airtel-money-yazindua-africa-bila.html)
- Mixx by Yas / Tigo Pesa (Tanzania): app-store listings found were in English only ("A secure way to send money, pay bills, top up airtime, and access quick loans") — **NO SOURCE FOUND** for a genuine Swahili-language app description string; the Tanzania-region store listing did not surface Swahili body copy in this search. [Google Play – Mixx Tanzania](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=tz.tigo.mfsapp&hl=en)
- M-Pesa's colloquial Swahili shorthand "**Pesa mkononi**" ("money in the hand") is in wide informal/vernacular use around M-Pesa as a concept, but I could not confirm it as Safaricom's own official tagline text on a current owned page — it shows up as a phrase in surrounding Swahili media and Facebook pages, not verified as first-party copy. **Treat as informal folk-shorthand, not confirmed brand copy.** [Facebook: Pesa Mkononi](https://www.facebook.com/pesa.mkononi/)

**Pattern**: where Swahili appears in real product-facing copy, it is short, uses everyday vocabulary (pole pole, mkononi), and is deployed as a named feature/plan rather than as marketing prose. Longer, more formal Swahili (huduma bunifu, kugusa maisha) shows up in press/PR quotes from executives, not in app UI or homepage headlines aimed at users.

### 4. Low-bandwidth / literacy conventions

Across the sample, several consistent conventions recur:

- **Numbers stated concretely, not rounded into abstraction**: "1%", "25 free transfers", "KSh 50,000", "90 days to 12 months" — precise numerals rather than "low fees" or "flexible terms." [wave.com](https://www.wave.com/en/) [kuda.com](https://kuda.com/) [OPay listing](https://apps.apple.com/ng/app/opay-beyond-banking/id1463776084)
- **Short, single-clause headlines**: "Mobile money, reinvented." "The digital bank built for Nigerians." "Smart Phone, Smart Money, Smart Choice." None of the homepages fetched used a headline over roughly 8 words. [wave.com](https://www.wave.com/en/) [kuda.com](https://kuda.com/) [m-kopa.com](https://m-kopa.com/)
- **Feature bullets, not paragraphs**: Wave and Kuda both structure the core offer as 4-6 short bulleted claims rather than descriptive prose — this matches the general finding from HCI research that textual interfaces are error-prone for low-literacy/novice users and that plain, simple sentence structure with fewer culture-specific references reduces cognitive load. [ACM: Designing mobile interfaces for novice and low-literacy users](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1959022.1959024)
- **Full spelling-out over abbreviation** in the primary claim, but numerals kept as digits: Sun King says "buy outright or spread the cost" rather than assuming the reader knows financing jargon like "amortize" or "installment plan" by name — it explains the mechanism in plain words, then names the product. [tz.sunking.com](https://tz.sunking.com/)
- **Visual/icon reliance over text** is a documented broader pattern in African low-literacy UX research (up to 72% preference for icons over written instructions in some tested contexts), though this is interface-design literature rather than brand-copy literature specifically. [Zenodo: Designing UIs for Low-Literacy Populations in African Contexts](https://zenodo.org/records/18769440)

### 5. What is embarrassed / avoided

- **Long, unbulleted paragraphs of self-description** are absent from every product homepage fetched — none of M-Pesa, Wave, Kuda, PiggyVest, Moniepoint, Flutterwave, or Sun King leads with prose; all lead with a short headline plus bullets.
- **Vague claims without a number attached** are avoided in the fee/speed category specifically — competitors in this space (Wave, OPay, Kuda) compete on stating an exact percentage or free-transfer count rather than saying "low fees," suggesting the market has learned that vague price language reads as evasive.
- **Overt charity/beneficiary framing directed at the user** is avoided in copy meant to close a transaction — even M-KOPA, which is the most donor-toned brand in the sample in its *impact reporting*, keeps its actual product headline consumer-facing ("Smart Phone, Smart Money, Smart Choice") and pushes the "unlocking financial inclusion" language into a separate "impact" section rather than the primary offer. [m-kopa.com](https://m-kopa.com/) This suggests a working convention: donor/impact language, if used at all, is segregated away from the transactional copy the user actually reads to decide whether to sign up.

### 6. Anti-patterns: copy written for a donor/foreign audience, not the user

Real, sourced examples where the language is aimed at investors, DFIs (development finance institutions), or a Western readership rather than the person actually using the product:

- UNDP South Africa: "**Bridging this divide is not just about connectivity; it's about unlocking futures, fostering innovation, and ensuring that no one is left behind in the digital age.**" This is institutional-development language — "no one left behind," "unlocking futures" — addressed to a policy/donor audience, not a consumer deciding whether to download an app. [aredgroup.com](https://aredgroup.com/empowering-e-citizens-bridging-africas-digital-divide-in-government-initiatives/)
- Flutterwave's own homepage tagline: "**Empowering businesses of all sizes with seamless payment solutions tailored for enterprises, startups, and emerging markets.**" ("empowering," "seamless," "emerging markets" in a single sentence) — three of the exact red-flag terms the brief names, on a real, live homepage. Worth noting Flutterwave's own product-level copy below this line ("Send money home to loved ones, sell online as a small business...") is plainer and more concrete — the donor-toned line sits specifically in the top-level positioning statement, which is the part most likely to have been written for investors reading a pitch deck rather than a user reading a signup page. [flutterwave.com](https://flutterwave.com/)
- Named and documented critique of the substance behind this style of language, from a Nigerian fintech insider: "**I've seen fintechs brag about 'connecting Africa' while charging 3 to 6% on every mobile money transaction. That's not inclusion. That's extortion smartly coiffed and dressed up as innovation.**" — a direct, sourced account of the gap between "inclusion/empowerment" marketing register and the pricing reality it's sometimes used to paper over. [dejiolowe.com](https://dejiolowe.com/2025/11/02/african-fintechs-are-robbing-the-poor-blind/) The same piece states plainly that "**these same fintechs often use the language of inclusion and empowerment to raise money from global investors and DFIs**" — i.e., the register is correctly diagnosed as investor-facing, not user-facing, by someone inside the industry.
- Scholarly confirmation of the same pattern at a structural level: coverage of FinTech in the African press skews toward "afro-optimism" framing tech as inherently a force for empowerment and inclusion, a framing academic critique says can mask "neo-colonial" power dynamics in how these platforms actually operate. [tandfonline.com: Afro-optimism and progressive modernity](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14747731.2023.2275816) [tandfonline.com: Neo-colonial credit](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17530350.2022.2028652)

**What makes these fail, specifically**: they name the abstraction ("inclusion," "empowerment," "the digital divide") instead of the concrete thing the user gets (a number, a speed, a place their money goes). Every successful consumer example in §2 does the opposite — it replaces the abstraction with a stated percentage, minute count, or currency figure. The donor-register copy is diagnosable by a simple test that recurs across sources here: would this sentence survive being read aloud to the actual user standing at a kiosk deciding whether to sign up? The failing examples above do not.

## Part 2 — Tone when a product is a memorial

### 1. Documented cases of companies/products carrying a deceased person's name or story

- **mDoc (Nigeria), founder Nneka Mobisson.** Her father, Oliver Mobisson, died in 2010 of a stroke she describes as "**premature and preventable**" due to lack of access to a care team; her grief and anger at seeing this repeated across Africa is described as the direct motivator for founding mDoc. Critically: **the company name itself does not reference her father** — mDoc is a functional/descriptive name. The one place personal naming *does* appear is inside the product: the chatbot is named **Kem**, "short for '*nkem*' which in the Igbo language means 'my own'" — a cultural/linguistic reference, not a literal memorial name. The grief origin story is disclosed prominently, but in a **third-party long-form profile** (a Gates Foundation "Ideas" feature, published under a headline that explicitly foregrounds grief), not on mDoc's own homepage, About page, or app-store copy. [gatesfoundation.org](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/ideas/articles/nneka-mobisson-mdoc-healthcare)
- **Charlotte's Web (US, cannabis/CBD).** The clearest counter-example: the product line is literally named after a real child, Charlotte Figi, whose severe epilepsy was treated by the strain that was renamed in her honor. Unlike mDoc, the naming is total and explicit — it's the brand's entire identity, not a sub-feature. Figi was alive when the name was adopted (renamed from "Hippie's Disappointment" to "Charlotte's Web" in her honor) and died in 2020; the company subsequently created a public "Charlotte Figi Family Grant Program" and Colorado declared an official "Charlotte Figi Day." This is a maximal-disclosure case: her story is core marketing material, covered in a CNN documentary, and the company leans into it publicly and repeatedly, including after her death. [Wikipedia: Charlotte's Web (cannabis)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte's_Web_(cannabis)) [PR Newswire: Charlotte Figi Family Grant Program](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/charlottes-web-celebrates-cbd-industrys-1st-decade-with-its-new-charlotte-figi-family-grant-program-301519948.html) [New Hope Network: Charlotte's death](https://www.newhope.com/industry-insights/charlotte-namesake-of-charlotte-s-web-hemp-cbd-brand-dies-from-covid-19)
- **Pauline Cosmetics (Kenya).** Reported as founded and named in honour of the founder's mother — found via a secondary business-profile listicle rather than the company's own site, so I could not independently verify how (or whether) this is stated on Pauline Cosmetics' own marketing. **Flagged as lower-confidence**: single secondary source. [Businessday NG](https://businessday.ng/bd-weekender/article/9-african-entrepreneurs-who-returned-home-to-build-successful-businesses-after-living-abroad/)

**Pattern across the two well-documented cases**: disclosure level tracks how load-bearing the personal story is to the *name itself*. Where the name is generic/functional (mDoc) and only a minor internal feature carries a personal reference (Kem), the grief story lives in press profiles, not owned brand copy. Where the personal name *is* the brand (Charlotte's Web), disclosure is maximal, repeated, and part of the core marketing asset. I found no documented case in this research of a product with the deceased's actual name on the marquee that then chose to keep that fact unstated on its own site — once the name itself is personal, hiding the story doesn't appear to be an available option; the choice becomes how much *more* than the name to say, not whether to say anything.

### 2. Is there a documented rule or professional consensus on disclosing personal tragedy in brand copy?

**No settled consensus was found — say so rather than assembling one.** What exists is a set of individually-argued, non-authoritative positions from marketing writers and one cyberpsychology/grief-adjacent commentator, not a codified professional standard (no equivalent of an industry body's guidance was located):

- A copywriter's argument that **grief should not be treated as strategy at all**: "grief cannot be branded... there are no winning strategies for tragedies, and the only way to prepare for them is by being predictably humane," and that it should be "about humanity, not marketing." [Medium: The Tragedy of Marketing Tragedy](https://medium.com/@tanveerrahman/the-tragedy-of-marketing-tragedy-45b088915dc)
- A sharper polemic against monetized grief, distinguishing between returning to normal work as a coping mechanism versus "**turning your spouse's death into a content schedule with a checkout link**" — explicitly framing the distinction as between authentic resilience and "opportunity capitalization." [The Daily Draft: Grief Isn't a Marketing Strategy](https://www.thedailydraftnewsletter.com/p/grief-isnt-a-marketing-strategy)
- A more procedural set of self-check questions (aimed at professionals using personal-loss narrative to build authority, not brand copy specifically, but transferable): does withholding context serve privacy or personal benefit; would the story be told the same way without financial/professional gain; does surviving something automatically justify claiming you can help others with it. [Nicole Longmire, "When grief becomes part of the brand"](https://inthepast7days.substack.com/p/when-grief-becomes-part-of-the-brand)

These three sources **agree directionally** (restraint over exploitation, humanity over funnel-building) but none constitutes a citable industry standard, and none addresses the specific case here — a name chosen once, years earlier, for reasons of the heart, that a stranger encounters incidentally on a functioning product's homepage. That specific configuration (a *naming* decision already made, rather than an ongoing grief-marketing *campaign*) sits outside what any of these sources actually discuss. **This gap should be treated as real, not papered over.**

### 3. Failure modes — where does saying it publicly go wrong?

Documented failure patterns, drawn from the sources above and general findings on "grief as brand":

- **Turning the story into a funnel.** The clearest named failure: content or copy structured so that the loss leads directly to a call-to-action or checkout — "a funeral speech becomes copywriting, and heartbreak becomes branding" is the phrase used to describe the tipping point. [Medium: The Tragedy of Marketing Tragedy](https://medium.com/@tanveerrahman/the-tragedy-of-marketing-tragedy-45b088915dc)
- **Using loss to manufacture unearned authority/credibility** — i.e., "I survived X, therefore trust me on Y" as a substitute for demonstrated competence, rather than as color on a founder's motivation. [inthepast7days.substack.com](https://inthepast7days.substack.com/p/when-grief-becomes-part-of-the-brand)
- **Ads that make the customer feel obliged or exploited by proximity to someone else's grief**: documented cases of bereavement charities and parents objecting when advertising used childhood-loss storylines to sell products, describing the experience as "upsetting" to see "grief wrapped up neatly as a sales pitch" — companies pulled the ads after criticism. [martech.org](https://martech.org/brands-death-social-media/)
- **The specific risk named in the brief — making a benefit-driven product look like a charity case** — is not directly documented in a named case study in this research (**NO SOURCE FOUND** for a specific African-market example of this exact failure), but it follows logically from the pattern established in Part 1 §5/§6: African consumer products that lean into "impact"/beneficiary language (UNDP-style, or M-KOPA's "unlocking financial inclusion" framing) do so in sections segregated from the transactional offer — suggesting that market practice already treats charity-adjacent framing as something to keep separate from, not blended into, the sales pitch. A memorial disclosure risks the same effect if placed where the user is deciding whether to trust the product's core function (finding a job) rather than where they're reading about who built it.

### 4. What this means for VOICE, not copy — concrete rules

Translating "dignity, warmth, no hype, no gimmicks, nothing that would embarrass her name" into checkable rules, drawing on the Part 1 findings for what actually works in this market plus the Part 2 failure-mode analysis:

**Words to avoid** (per Part 1 §6, these already fail on their own terms in this market, independent of the memorial question): *empowering, leveraging, unlocking potential, bridging the divide, journey, ecosystem, beneficiary, impact* (as a noun applied to a person), *inspiring*. None of these appear in the winning consumer copy surveyed in Part 1; they are the donor/press register, not the user register.

**Sentence shapes to use**: short, imperative or plainly declarative, one claim per sentence, numbers as numerals — matching the pattern found across Wave, Kuda, Sun King, and Moniepoint (Part 1 §1, §4). A memorial's dignity is better served by restraint executed *consistently* — the same plainness applied to an error message as to a success message — than by a single solemn paragraph that then sits next to hype-toned copy elsewhere on the site. Inconsistency (solemn About page, hyped-up pricing page) would read as more exploitative than either register alone.

**Claims to refuse**: no claim of "changing lives" or "transforming futures" for the jobseeker — the failure-mode research in §3 shows this is exactly the language that (a) reads as donor/press-facing rather than user-facing per Part 1, and (b) is the register most associated with turning a personal story into unearned authority. State what the product does (surfaces jobs, tracks applications, notifies the user) — not what it promises to mean for someone's life.

**Tone under failure/error states**: no cuteness, no jokes, no exclamation marks. An error state ("no jobs matched your search," "this listing has closed") should be as plain and respectful as it would be if the founder himself were reading it back — flat, useful, no apology theater ("Oops!"). This is the single most concrete, enforceable link between "memorial" and "voice": a product named for someone should not, in its worst moments, sound flippant.

**Tone in celebration states**: warm but restrained — a hire confirmed, a first job found — is the moment closest to the emotional register a memorial implies, but the rule should still be: celebrate the user's outcome, not the product's role in it. "You got the interview" beats "We're so proud to have helped you get here."

**A general enforceable test**: before publishing any line of copy, ask whether it would read the same if the product had no personal history behind it at all. If a sentence only "works" because the reader knows about the sister, it's leaning on the memorial rather than earning its place on its own — which is also, independently, the exact failure mode identified in §3.

### 5. Recommendation

For a stranger in Dar es Salaam reading the landing page cold, with no knowledge of the founder: **the memorial should be unstated on the primary landing page, and belong instead on an About/Story page one click away — named plainly, disclosed briefly, and not decorated.**

Reasoning, tied directly to the research above:

- Part 1 shows the entire winning register in this market is transactional and concrete (§1–§4): a landing page's job is to answer "what does this do, what does it cost, how fast" — the same job Wave, Kuda, and Sun King's homepages do. A memorial statement on that page competes with, and by the failure-mode analysis in §3 risks undermining, that transactional trust.
- The two well-documented naming cases in §1 diverge exactly on this axis: mDoc (name not personal) keeps the story off owned brand copy entirely; Charlotte's Web (name *is* the person) discloses maximally because the name demands an explanation the moment someone asks "why is it called that." A career product named after a sister sits closer to the Charlotte's Web case — the name itself invites the question — so *fully* unstated risks looking evasive if a curious user searches. The right level is therefore not silence, but **quiet, one-click availability**: present for whoever looks, absent from whoever doesn't.
- No consensus source recommends the opposite (loud, front-page disclosure) for a working commercial product as opposed to a memorial-first cause; the sources in §2–§3 lean uniformly toward restraint.

**If stated, suggested placement**: a short paragraph on an About/Our Story page (not the homepage, not the footer of every page), roughly:

> *English:* "[Product name] is named for [her name], [founder's relationship] — who [one grounded, specific detail, not a eulogy]. This is a jobs platform, built for people looking for work in Tanzania. We hope it does her name credit."
>
> *Swahili:* "[Jina la bidhaa] limepewa jina la [jina lake], [uhusiano wa mwanzilishi] — ambaye [maelezo mafupi, mahususi]. Hii ni jukwaa la ajira, lililojengwa kwa ajili ya watu wanaotafuta kazi Tanzania. Tunatumaini linalistahi jina lake."

This wording is a **draft for the owner to accept, edit, or reject** — the specific biographical detail, the exact relationship phrasing, and whether "hope it does her name credit" is the right closing register are all judgment calls only the founder can make; a stranger-researcher should not be the one deciding how a family's grief gets worded. The one structural recommendation I'd hold to regardless of wording: keep it off the page the user lands on to decide whether to search for a job, and keep it short enough that it doesn't ask the reader to participate in the founder's mourning in order to use the product.
